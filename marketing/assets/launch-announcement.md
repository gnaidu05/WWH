# Hire an entire company of AI specialists in about 60 seconds

You already do this every day, and you already know it doesn't work: you open your AI coding tool and type *"act as a senior backend engineer."* Or *"you are a world-class growth marketer."* Then you paste your problem and hope the model fills in the other 90% — who this person is, how they actually work, and what they're supposed to hand you when they're done.

It fills in that 90% with an average. A plausible, confident, forgettable average. That's the problem with a generic prompt: it has no spine. It doesn't know whether it owes you a schema, a test plan, a landing page, or a threat model, so it gives you a little of everything and a commitment to nothing.

**The Agency is 85 handwritten AI specialist agents that fix exactly that.** It's an open-source library, MIT licensed and free, and you install it into the AI coding tool you already use — Claude Code, Cursor, Copilot, Codex, Gemini, Windsurf, Aider, and more — with a single command: `scripts/install.sh`.

## A prompt is a suggestion. An agent is a contract.

Every agent in The Agency is written by hand to pin down four things a generic prompt leaves blank:

- **Who it is** — a real role with a point of view, not "a helpful assistant."
- **What it specializes in** — the specific surface it owns, and the parts it deliberately doesn't.
- **How it works** — an actual workflow, step by step, not vibes.
- **What it owes you back** — the concrete deliverable. A migration file. A test matrix. A content calendar. Something you can open and use.

"Act as a developer" gives you a stranger who might do anything. A `backend-architect` agent gives you someone who knows it's responsible for the data model and the service boundaries, works through them in order, and hands back an actual design — not three paragraphs about how important architecture is.

That's the whole difference: a suggestion versus a contract. You stop describing the specialist you wish you had, and you start invoking one that already knows its job.

Engineering, Design, Product, Marketing, Sales, Security, Finance, Testing & QA, Game Dev, Data & AI, DevOps & Infra, Operations, Research, Mapping & GIS, Content & Editorial, and Growth. It's a company, not a Swiss Army knife — which brings us to the one instruction that matters most on day one.

## Don't install all 85. Start with seven.

Eighty-five specialists is a company, and nobody onboards a whole company on a Tuesday. So we picked a starter squad that covers a real product from idea to sanity check:

`rapid-prototyper` · `backend-architect` · `ai-engineer` · `whimsy-injector` · `growth-hacker` · `content-creator` · `reality-checker`

That's enough to ship something and enough to keep yourself honest. Add the other 78 when you actually have a job for them — not before.

## The payoff: an orchestrator that runs the room

Individually, each agent is a sharp tool. Together they need someone to point them at a goal — and that's the `agents-orchestrator`. You hand it an outcome, and it runs the specialists toward it: the prototyper builds, the backend-architect hardens, the growth-hacker finds the loop, the content-creator writes it up, the reality-checker calls out what's not actually true yet. You direct; the company works.

It's the difference between having a folder of clever prompts and having a team you can actually delegate to.
